# Looking after hands and lungs

Clay dust is the thing about pottery nobody warns you about when you begin. Dried clay and glaze materials contain fine crystalline silica, and the particles that matter are too small to see. Inhaled over years, they scar the lungs. Silicosis is an old potter's disease, and it doesn't heal.

So the studio rules are:

- **Never sweep.** A broom just lifts dust into the air. Wet-mop the floor and sponge surfaces every day.
- **Keep it wet.** Scraps go into the reclaim bucket wet; I don't sand bone-dry pots unless I'm outside with a mask.
- **Mask for the dusty jobs**: mixing dry glaze, cleaning the [[Electric kiln]], sieving ash. A properly fitted respirator with P3 filters, not a paper dust mask.
- **Aprons that don't hold dust.** Canvas and denim trap it; I wear a coated one and wash it weekly.
- **No eating or drinking** at the glazing bench.

Hands need care too. Clay pulls the oil out of skin, and some glaze ingredients are harsh. I wear nitrile gloves when mixing glazes or washing ash for [[Ash glaze]], and use a thick balm at night on split fingertips after long throwing days.

Then there's heat: welding goggles for looking into the kiln at cone 10, and leather gloves for unloading.

None of this is dramatic. It's just the daily clean-down in my [[Studio routine]], done for thirty years so that I can still be throwing at seventy. See also [[Reclaiming clay]].
