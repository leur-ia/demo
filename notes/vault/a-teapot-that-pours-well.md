# A teapot that pours well

It took me eleven teapots to make one I'd put on the table in front of guests. The shape is easy; the pour is hard.

That drip running down the belly after you tip it back comes down to the tip of the spout. What I've learned:

- **Sharp lip**: the edge of the spout should be cut thin and crisp, almost knife-like. A rounded, thick lip lets the tea cling and creep back underneath.
- **Height**: the tip must sit higher than the rim of the body, or it overflows when filled.
- **Angle**: I cut the end with a slight downward bevel, so the stream breaks off cleanly when you tilt it back.
- **Holes**: a strainer of many small holes, drilled at leather hard, with a total area larger than the spout opening, so the flow is steady and doesn't gurgle.

The spout is thrown separately as a small tapered cone, cut at an angle, and joined when both parts are the same dryness. It will twist slightly in the kiln as it unwinds from the throwing spiral, so I attach it turned a few degrees to compensate.

Balance matters too. With the pot full, the handle should sit so your wrist does the pouring, not your fingers; I make mine with the method in [[Pulling handles]]. The lid needs a deep flange so it stays put when tipped; see [[Lids and galleries]].

It's now the pot on the table at every one of my [[Tea mornings]].
