# Glaze chemistry basics

I'm not a chemist, but I've learned enough to stop guessing. Every glaze is a balance of three kinds of ingredient:

- **Silica**: the glass former. On its own it would melt at around 1700 °C, far beyond my kiln.
- **Fluxes**: calcium, potassium, sodium, magnesium. They lower the melting point so the silica melts at cone 10.
- **Alumina**: the stiffener. It keeps the melted glass from running off the pot and gives it body.

The ratio between silica and alumina decides whether a glaze is glossy or matt: more silica, more gloss. The choice of flux changes colour and texture; magnesium gives a buttery surface, calcium a harder, brighter one.

Colour comes from small additions of metal oxides:

- iron oxide: 1–2% for [[Celadon]], 8–10% for [[Tenmoku]];
- cobalt: 0.5% is already a strong blue;
- copper: red in reduction, but only if the kiln cooperates.

A few rules of thumb pinned on the wall:

1. If a glaze grows a web of fine lines, it's shrinking more than the clay. Add silica or swap sodium for something tamer ([[Crazing]]).
2. If it beads up and leaves bare patches, it has too much raw clay or went on too thick ([[Crawling]]).
3. Always change one thing at a time and fire it on [[Test tiles]].
