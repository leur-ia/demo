# Crazing

Crazing is a fine web of lines in the glaze, sometimes visible straight out of the kiln, sometimes appearing weeks later with a faint tick-tick on the shelf. It happens when the glaze shrinks more than the clay underneath as they cool, so the glass is stretched until it gives.

On decorative pieces it can be beautiful; [[Raku]] depends on it. On a mug, it's a problem: the lines can hold tea stains and bacteria, and they weaken the pot.

How I fix it:

- add silica to the glaze, a few percent at a time;
- swap high-expansion fluxes like sodium and potassium for calcium, magnesium or boron;
- fire a little hotter, so the clay body matures and tightens;
- apply the glaze thinner.

My [[Celadon]] is prone to it because it's rich in feldspar, which brings in a lot of sodium and potassium. I've reduced it with 5% extra silica, at the cost of a slightly less glassy surface.

The opposite fault, a glaze that's too tight and pushes on the clay until slivers flake off rims, is called shivering. It's rarer and more dangerous. Both are about the fit between glaze and body, which [[Glaze chemistry basics]] explains. I check fit by boiling a test mug and then dropping it in ice water, a harsh [[Test tiles|test]] that shows the lines with a dab of ink.
