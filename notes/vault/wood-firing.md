# Wood firing

Twice a year I join a group of potters who share a big anagama, a tunnel kiln dug into a hillside. It takes three days and nights of stoking, in shifts, and about four tonnes of split pine.

Nothing about it is precise. The flame travels the length of the chamber and carries fine flying cinders that settle on the shoulders of the pots and melt into a natural glassy coat. On the side facing the fire, pots come out with drips and runs the colour of honey and moss; on the sheltered side, the bare clay turns a flushed orange or a dry pewter.

What I take up there:

- unglazed pots with strong shoulders, so the melted deposit has somewhere to land;
- [[Tea bowls]] set on little wads of clay, which leave scars on the foot that people love;
- a few pots dipped in [[Shino]], which goes wonderfully wild in that atmosphere.

The stoking rhythm becomes a kind of meditation. Every five minutes, two or three splits of wood through the firebox door, watch the flame roll out of the chimney, wait. At night the kiln glows through every gap in the bricks.

We fire to cone 12 at the front and barely cone 9 at the back, which is why placement matters so much (see [[Firing schedules and cones]]). The cool-down alone takes four days. My notes from the last firing are in [[Journal — 22 September]].
