# Crawling

It's the fault that makes a pot look as if the glaze has changed its mind. In the kiln, the molten glaze pulls back on itself into thick rolled beads and islands, leaving patches of bare clay between them. The edges of the bald spots are rounded and fat, not sharp, which is how I know it happened while the glaze was melting rather than being knocked off afterwards.

The causes are almost always in the raw glaze layer, before the firing even begins:

- **Too much raw clay** in the recipe. It shrinks as it dries, the glaze coat splits like a dry riverbed, and in the fire each piece draws up into a bead.
- **Applied too thick**, especially on a second dip. [[Shino]] is the worst offender.
- **Dust or grease** on the bisque. Hand cream, a dusty shelf: the glaze can't grip.
- **Handling** the glazed pot before it's dry, which lifts the coat away in small flakes.

What I do now:

1. Wipe every bisqued pot with a damp sponge before glazing.
2. Calcine part of the clay in high-clay glazes (bake it in the bisque first) so it shrinks less.
3. Add a little gum to thick glazes to help them hold.
4. Rub over any hairline shrinkage lines in the dry glaze with a fingertip.

Test on [[Test tiles]] first, always. A whole kiln of mugs with bare patches taught me that, in [[Journal — 19 April]]. The underlying chemistry is in [[Glaze chemistry basics]].
