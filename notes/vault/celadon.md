# Celadon

My celadon is the glaze I'm proudest of and the one that took longest to get right. It's a simple, glassy recipe with just 1.5% red iron oxide, and the magic is entirely in the fire. In [[Gas kiln reduction|reduction]], the iron changes state and the glaze turns a cool grey-blue, like shallow sea water over pale sand. Where it pools in a carved line or the bottom of a bowl, it deepens to something close to the colour of old bottle glass.

Fired in oxidation in the [[Electric kiln]], the same bucket gives a flat, pale straw-yellow. It's a good lesson in how much the atmosphere matters.

What I've learned:

- it wants thickness, applied by dipping for a count of three;
- carving or stamping under it makes it come alive, because the glaze collects in the hollows;
- on [[Porcelain]] it's colder and bluer, on my [[Stoneware body]] warmer and greyer;
- too much iron (anything over 2%) and it heads toward olive and brown.

It is a glassy, high-expansion glaze, so a fine network of lines sometimes appears on it days after firing. I've half made peace with that; see [[Crazing]].

> The colour isn't in the bucket. It's in the flame.
